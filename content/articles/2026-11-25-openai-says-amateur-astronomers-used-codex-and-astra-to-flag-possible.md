OpenAI has reported that two amateur astronomers turned to its Codex and Astra tools to comb through public telescope archives for evidence of distant worlds. Each of their searches produced potential exoplanets, and those candidates have now been handed to professionals for an independent review.

Kevin Bond, an engineer at OpenAI, ran Codex over observations of roughly 100,000 stars gathered by the Kepler telescope. His program raised more than 200 signals, 10 of which matched planets that were already confirmed, which gave him confidence in the method. Two stars drew particular attention. An astronomer at the SETI Institute ran a separate search and reported finding those same two signals.

Ethan Byrd, who founded the email infrastructure start-up Primitive, relied on Astra to examine data from close to 19 million stars, drawing largely on NASA's TESS mission. His initial sweep yielded 49 candidates. One of the strongest has been forwarded to scientists working on TESS, where it is queued for deeper analysis, and two further candidates are set to be submitted.

OpenAI stressed that verifying a planet can take months or even years. Researchers normally rule out other explanations for a dimming star, such as a neighbouring star crossing in front of it or shifts in a telescope's temperature. They may also measure the star's slight wobble, known as radial velocity, and results generally appear in peer-reviewed journals.

The company also highlighted NASA's Exoplanet Watch and the AAVSO's Exoplanet Observing Program as routes for members of the public who want to get involved.
